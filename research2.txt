# Why Chimera's render is dark at 2.5–4.5 kHz and sounds "almost human": diagnosis and fix list

The dark band at about 2.5–4.5 kHz is most likely caused by a source spectrum that is too steep plus a tract that has no small laryngeal resonator, not by too few tube sections. Your loss function makes this worse. A plain log-mel difference with a flat, slightly wrong F0 makes harmonic misalignment the biggest error term. That pushes the search toward a dull, "safe" render (brightness gene 0.241, smooth_wave 0.094). Fix pitch and the loss first, because they are cheap. Then open up the source tilt. Then add an epilarynx (the short, narrow tube just above the vocal folds), time-varying keyframes, and a real frication noise source.

## TL;DR

- **Most likely causes of the missing band, in order:** (1) the glottal source is too dull. That means a high Rd, too much smoothing, or no flow-derivative / lip-radiation boost. (2) The tract has no narrow epilarynx / laryngeal-cavity section, which is what creates the 3–3.7 kHz "ring" cluster. (3) The lips use a fixed reflection coefficient instead of a frequency-dependent radiation load. 1D tube models are only valid up to about 4–5 kHz. Above that, the plane-wave assumption breaks, so expect a residual gap there that no gene can close.
- **The loss is misleading the optimizer.** A 1–2% F0 error moves harmonic 10 and above by a whole mel band. Log compression then makes the valleys between harmonics count as much as the peaks. Fix F0 to the recording's own contour (pYIN/CREPE/RMVPE). Then fit the envelope with a smoothed (cepstrally liftered) spectral loss plus a multi-resolution STFT loss, with per-band normalization. Fit in stages: timing → pitch → articulation → source. Use CMA-ES rather than a plain GA.
- **The "uncanny" feel comes mostly from missing time variation.** There is one static vowel per segment, a nearly flat F0, a voiced sound where a fricative should be, and a fixed jitter/shimmer level. Add per-segment F0 targets (target-approximation style), 5–8 articulation keyframes for the 1 s clip with time constants of roughly 15–75 ms, and a constriction noise source driven by flow. Then run the seven diagnostics below to confirm where the gap really is before building anything expensive.

## Key Findings

### 1. The tube itself is probably not the main high-frequency limit, but its endings and missing side parts are

- **Section count and sample rate.** The original Pink Trombone (PT) uses 44 cylindrical sections. It runs each one as a half-sample delay, processing at twice the audio rate. At 48 kHz this gives a tract length of about 44 × 350 / (2 × 48000) ≈ 0.16 m. That is a typical adult male tract, as Südholt et al. (DAFx 2023) state. Each section is then about 3.6 mm long. That is fine enough to place F1–F5 correctly below 5 kHz. Kelly-Lochbaum theory only needs about 9 sections at 16 kHz for a 17.5 cm tract (Coleman, Oxford phonetics notes). **Important check:** in PT-style code the tract length is fixed by the section count *and* the sample rate. If Chimera runs at 44.1 kHz, the tract becomes about 0.175 m. If it runs at 22.05 kHz with the same 44 sections, it becomes about 0.35 m, which is twice too long. Every formant then drops by about half. The optimizer would try to "fix" this with body_scale and the vowel genes, and it would never reach the right F3–F5.
- **The plane-wave limit.** All 1D models assume sound travels as flat (plane) waves along the tube. Blandin and co-workers (ICSV 2021) describe plane-wave models as used "in the frequency range for which the plane wave assumption is valid (up to 4-5 kHz)", and their Interspeech 2021 paper puts it at "about 5 kHz". Above that, cross-modes (sound bouncing side to side) appear, which depend on the 3D shape. So above about 5 kHz, expect a mismatch no gene can fix. Your band (2.5–4.5 kHz) is mostly *below* that limit. That means it should be fixable inside a 1D model.
- **The missing laryngeal resonator (singer's-formant-like cluster).** Sundberg (JASA 1974) explained the "singing formant": the larynx tube (epilarynx) acts as its own small resonator when the pharynx is at least about 6 times wider than the larynx tube opening. That clusters F3, F4 and F5 near 2.8–3 kHz in adult males. In children it sits near 4 kHz because their tubes are smaller. MRI work on singers has not always confirmed the exact 6:1 ratio in living people. Mainka et al. (PLOS ONE 2015), scanning 13 male singing students, found "an average lowering of the larynx by 8 mm and an increase of the hypopharyngeal cross-sectional area (+ 21.9%) and volume (+ 16.8%)", while "changes in the analyzed epilaryngeal portion of the vocal tract were not significant." Either way, the acoustic recipe is the same: a narrow 2–3 cm tube above the glottis opening into a wide lower pharynx. Even in plain speech, the laryngeal cavity makes its own resonance. Kitamura et al. (JASA 2006; five vowels, three male speakers) report that "the resonance appears at the frequency region from 3.0 to 3.7kHz when the glottis is closed and disappears when it is open." Takemoto et al. (JASA 2006) traced F4 to this cavity. A PT-style tract has a smooth, simple base diameter at the glottis end, with no narrow epilarynx and no wide hypopharynx. Your genes `body_pharynx` and `vowel_epilarynx` may only change the diameter a little. If the narrowest allowed epilarynx is not at most about 1/6 of the hypopharynx area, the cluster cannot form. **This is the best match to a persistent horizontal deficit band that does not change with the vowel.**
- **Piriform fossae (two small side pockets beside the larynx).** These act as side branches. They add a dip (anti-resonance) at about 4–5 kHz (Dang & Honda 1997; Delvaux & Howard 2014). VocalTractLab models them with a total volume of 1.5 cm³ and a length of 2.5 cm (VTL 2.3 manual). They *remove* energy around 4–5 kHz and sharpen the singer's cluster below it. So they are not why your render is too dark. In Birkholz & Drechsel's listening test (Speech Communication 2021), the changes "could be perceived, but they were less preferred by the listener (at least for the piriform fossae and transvelar acoustic coupling)", and with a more tense voice there was no preference either way. Treat them as a late, optional refinement.
- **Lip radiation.** Real radiation acts like a piston in a baffle. Low frequencies reflect back into the tract almost completely. High frequencies radiate out more. The pressure output rises at about +6 dB per octave, which is a first-order differentiator (Airaksinen et al., Interspeech 2014). PT uses a constant lip reflection of −0.85 and a glottal reflection of +0.75. This appears in the PT constants of a C++ port. The subagent, working from memory of the original JS, also reported that no extra lip-radiation filter is applied. PT gets its +6 dB/octave instead by generating the glottal flow *derivative* (Südholt et al. say the lip radiation is "combined with G to form a model of the glottal flow derivative"). **If Chimera feeds the tract with glottal flow rather than its derivative, or if `smooth_wave` low-passes the derivative, the render loses about 6 dB per octave.** That alone is about 20 dB less at 4 kHz than at 400 Hz.
- **Wall vibration and viscous losses** are not the main suspects. Wall impedance mainly affects frequencies below about 800 Hz (Birkholz, ICASSP 2022). Viscous losses widen high formants, but your `viscous_scale` is already low (0.160).
- **Nose and sinuses.** These add dips, not peaks. They matter for nasal sounds and are unlikely to explain a band that is dark on every vowel.

### 2. The glottal source decides the slope, and your genes suggest it is set too dull

- **What the LF model does.** The Liljencrants–Fant (LF) model shapes each glottal pulse. Its single shape parameter Rd runs from tense (low Rd) to breathy (high Rd). Low Rd raises the cut-off frequency of the spectral tilt, so the highs get louder. High Rd lowers it, so the voice gets darker (Fant 1995; Degottex/Perrotin et al. JASA 2021). The return phase (the short recovery after the closing peak) behaves like a first-order low-pass filter with a cut-off of Fa = 1/(2π·Ta) (Fant 1995). PT maps tenseness to Rd = 3·(1 − tenseness), clamped to 0.5–2.7. It estimates Rd from H1−H2 = −7.6 + 11.1·Rd, where H1−H2 is the level difference between the first two harmonics in dB (Südholt et al., citing Fant).
- **Rosenberg / KLGLOTT88** pulses are smooth and close gently. Their spectrum has no sharp closing event, so they fall off faster at high frequencies than an LF pulse with a short return phase. PT's own source is the LF model rebuilt each period, plus noise. PT aspiration noise is scaled by (1 − √tenseness)·0.3 (sndkit glottis docs).
- **Probable meaning of your genes.** Check these against your code.
  - `brightness` ≈ a spectral-tilt control. Usually this is the return-phase time Ta (or Ra = Ta/T0), or a post-filter. Low = dark.
  - `sharpness_add` ≈ how sudden the closing event is. That is the negative peak Ee relative to the flow peak, or Rk.
  - `skew_add` ≈ pulse asymmetry (Rk, or the speed quotient). More skew means a sharper closure and more highs.
  - `open_quotient_add` ≈ Oq, the share of each cycle the glottis is open. Higher Oq gives more H1 and a darker sound.
  - `smooth_wave` ≈ a low-pass or smoothing on the pulse. Any smoothing removes highs directly.
  - `chink_scale` ≈ a constant leak through a gap at the back of the glottis (glottal chink). It adds DC flow, more breath noise and a steeper tilt.
- **Are the ranges clipping the optimum?** `brightness` = 0.241 and `smooth_wave` = 0.094 sit near the low edges of their ranges. Most other genes sit mid-range. A low `smooth_wave` is good if 0 means "no smoothing". A low `brightness` with a dark render, though, means the search found that *more* brightness made the loss *worse*. That points to the loss (see Finding 4) or to brightness landing on the wrong formants, not to a voice that really is dark. Run Diagnostic 7 to see whether the population piles up at a bound.
- **Source–tract interaction.** In real voices, the tract's air load skews the glottal flow pulse. The cut-off gets sharper and the highs get stronger. The laryngeal-cavity resonance also switches on and off within each cycle (Kitamura et al. 2006). A pure "LF pulse in, tract out" model misses both. A cheap stand-in is a flow-dependent glottal reflection, or a slightly sharper closure when the tract load is high. Pitch-synchronous aspiration also matters. PT gates its noise with the glottal phase. Your `aspiration_scale` should be modulated by the glottal flow, not added as steady noise.

### 3. Noise and consonants: a voiced-only render cannot match a fricative

- Frication is turbulent noise made just downstream of a narrow constriction. It starts when the Reynolds number passes a critical value: about 1700 (Elie & Laprie) or 1800 (Birkholz 2006). Its amplitude grows roughly with the cube of the flow (Stevens). Models place a dipole noise source at the downstream end of the constriction, or at the teeth for /s/ and /ʃ/ (Birkholz 2006). The cavity in front of the constriction mostly shapes the spectrum.
- PT's turbulence noise is white noise band-passed at 1 kHz with Q = 0.5 (Faust pinktrombone.lib docs). Its aspiration filter is reportedly about 500 Hz, Q = 0.5. Both are low-centred. That suits PT's purpose, but it cannot produce the broad 2–8 kHz energy of a real fricative.
- Chimera has **no constriction event in time and no consonant model**. So the optimizer can only fill the noisy region with voiced harmonics. That is exactly what you see. A glottal chink also matters. Elie & Laprie (ICASSP 2016) show that voiced fricatives need a chink big enough to keep enough DC flow for turbulence. A very large chink devoices the sound.

### 4. The loss: why log-mel L1 punishes a near-miss in pitch and rewards dullness

- **Worked example (my own calculation).** At F0 = 200 Hz, a 2% error is 4 Hz. Harmonic 10 (2 kHz) then moves 40 Hz and harmonic 20 (4 kHz) moves 80 Hz. These shifts are about the size of one mel band at those frequencies. So the peaks of one signal land on the valleys of the other. That gives the red/blue horizontal stripes. Log compression turns a 30–40 dB peak-to-valley swing into a big error, *even though the two spectral envelopes match*. Making the render duller or noisier smears the harmonics and *lowers* this error. Stripe error therefore drives the search toward a dark render.
- **Published evidence.** Spectral losses are poor at fitting oscillator frequency. They have local minima at harmonic multiples. Pitch conditioning with CREPE splits frequency estimation from timbre estimation (Smith, CCRMA ASA 2025 handout). Schwär & Müller ("Multi-Scale Spectral Loss Revisited", IEEE Signal Processing Letters 2023) report that "the MSS loss often fails to provide informative gradients" when the learned parameters control the frequency of synthesis components. In Südholt et al. (DAFx 2023), a GA and particle swarm using mel-spectrogram MSE on PT lost a listening test to a method that inverse-filters the recording and fits only the tract frequency response (p < 0.001, 18 valid listeners).
- **What to use instead.** First fix F0 from the recording. Then combine: (a) a liftered-cepstrum or smoothed-envelope loss, which compares envelopes, not harmonics; (b) a multi-resolution STFT loss (Yamamoto et al. 2019: spectral convergence + log-magnitude, over several FFT sizes); (c) per-band normalization, so the quiet 2.5–5 kHz band counts as much as the loud low bands; (d) small extra terms for spectral tilt (H1−H2, and energy ratios such as 0–1 kHz vs 2–4 kHz), the harmonic-to-noise ratio per frame, and the gap between LPC formant tracks F1–F4; (e) a light DTW (dynamic time warping) alignment, or a timing-only stage, so pause positions do not dominate.
- **Optimizer.** CMA-ES is the standard for continuous genes in [0, 1]. Instrumental (arXiv 2026) used population λ = 40 and σ₀ = 0.15. Its multi-start runs (8×) improved results by 29%. Adding loosely constrained parameters made CMA-ES *worse*: it exploited physically implausible settings. So add genes in stages, each with a tight, physical range.

### 5. Time-varying control: how published systems do it

- VocalTractLab (VTL) uses **gestural scores**. These are tiers of gestures, each with a duration, a target and a time constant. Supraglottal targets are approached through a sixth-order critically damped system. The VTL manual suggests about 15 ms as a starting time constant for supraglottal gestures. Earlier measurements put tongue movements at about 75 ms (Birkholz, Interspeech 2007). F0 gestures follow the target-approximation (TA/qTA) model of Prom-on, Xu & Thipakorn. Each target is a straight line with a slope, approached smoothly, which naturally produces glides and the rise-then-fall you see. Coarticulation (how neighbouring sounds bend each other) uses context-dependent consonant targets, blended between /aCa/, /iCi/ and /uCu/ (VTL 2.3 manual).
- PT itself moves the tract toward its target at a speed limit of about 15 cm/s (`movementSpeed = 15`, from a C++ port of PT). Krug et al. (JASA 2021) showed that adding microprosody, meaning small F0 dips and rises tied to consonants, audibly improves articulatory synthesis. This is part of the "uncanny" fix.
- **Minimal keyframe set for your 1 s clip (recommended):**
  - **F0:** 2 TA targets per voiced segment, each with a level and a slope, so 6–8 targets. Or simply use the extracted F0 contour.
  - **Articulation:** one vowel target per segment (3–4), plus a start and end target for the formant glides in segments 2 and 4. That is 5–8 keyframes, each a full vowel_* vector, with one shared time constant gene (10–80 ms).
  - **Constriction:** one event with onset, offset, location (index along the tract), minimum area and noise gain.
  - **Loudness:** onset and offset times plus a level per segment, or simply the recording's RMS envelope.
  - This is roughly 60–90 genes. It is only workable with staged fitting and with F0/loudness taken from the recording.

## Diagnostic experiments (run these before building fixes 5–10)

1. **Long-term average spectrum (LTAS) ratio.** Compute the LTAS of the voiced frames only (Welch, 2048-point FFT, Hann window) for the recording and the render, both RMS-normalized. Plot 20·log10(rec/render) against log frequency. *Expected:* a straight upward line (about +6 dB/octave) means a tilt or source problem, so do fix 3. A bump of 10–20 dB centred near 3–3.5 kHz on top of a flat line means a missing resonator, so do fix 5. Both together are likely.
2. **Swap in the recording's F0 and envelope.** Re-render the current best genes with the recording's pYIN F0 contour and RMS envelope. Change nothing else. *Expected:* the stripes and much of the loss disappear. Re-running the search from there should move `brightness` upward. If it does, the loss was hiding brightness (confirms fix 2).
3. **Glottal inverse filtering.** Run IAIF or GFM-IAIF (Perrotin's MATLAB code; DisVoice or Aalto Aparat in Python/GUI) on 3–4 steady vowel frames. Compare the estimated flow derivative and its spectrum with Chimera's source at the fitted genes. *Expected:* if the recording's source spectrum is 6–12 dB stronger at 3 kHz, the source is the bottleneck. Südholt et al. found that inverse filtering adds error (tenseness MAE rose from 0.013 to 0.057 on PT's own audio), so compare slopes, not fine detail.
4. **LPC formant tracking.** Track F1–F5 in the recording (Praat Burg, 5–5.5 formants up to 5–5.5 kHz). Then sweep Chimera's vowel and body genes across their full ranges and record the reachable F3–F5. *Expected:* if the recording has F3–F5 bunched within about 1 kHz near 3 kHz and Chimera's F4/F5 never come closer than about 1.5 kHz, the tract geometry cannot make the cluster (fix 5).
5. **High-shelf and radiation A/B test.** Apply a fixed +6 dB/octave pre-emphasis (1 − 0.97 z⁻¹), then a +6/+10 dB high-shelf at 2.5 kHz, to the best render. Re-score the loss and listen. *Expected:* if most of the band closes with a simple shelf, it is tilt (fixes 3/6). If a peaking EQ at about 3.2 kHz (Q ≈ 2) closes it better than a shelf, it is a resonance (fix 5).
6. **Sample rate, section count and low-pass check.** Print the fs, the section count and the implied tract length (n·c/(2·fs) for half-sample models). Excite the tract with an impulse in a neutral shape and plot the transfer function. *Expected:* about 0.16–0.175 m, with formants near 500, 1500, 2500, 3500 and 4500 Hz for a uniform tube. Also look for any resampler or "smoothing" low-pass that cuts off below about 8 kHz.
7. **Bound-hugging check.** Log all genes for the top 10% of each generation. Plot histograms. *Expected:* any gene whose mass sits within 0.05 of a bound (watch `brightness`, `smooth_wave`, `fry`, `roughness`, `tremolo_db`, `chink_scale`) needs a wider or re-mapped range. Re-run with the range doubled in that direction and compare the loss.

## Recommended loss recipe (concrete)

- **Stage 0 (timing):** fit `time_shift_s` and the envelope by cross-correlating RMS envelopes, or with DTW on frame energy.
- **Stage 1 (pitch):** fix F0 to the extracted contour, with only a small offset gene.
- **Stage 2 (articulation):** the loss is the envelope loss (liftered cepstrum or a smoothed log-mel with a 300–500 Hz smoothing kernel) plus the formant-track loss. Source genes stay fixed at defaults.
- **Stage 3 (source):** add MR-STFT, the tilt (H1−H2, band ratios) and HNR terms. Free the source genes and the noise genes.
- **Throughout:** normalize the log-mel per band, cap per-bin errors (Huber) so a few stripes cannot dominate, and use CMA-ES (λ ≈ 32–64, σ₀ ≈ 0.15) with 4–8 restarts.

## Caveats

- I could not read Chimera's code. The gene meanings in Finding 2 are typical mappings. Check them against the code.
- Some PT constants come from a C++ port and from a subagent's memory of the original JS: lip reflection −0.85, glottal reflection 0.75, aspiration filter about 500 Hz, no output radiation filter. The 1 kHz/Q 0.5 turbulence filter, the 44 sections, the Rd mapping and clamp, and the aspiration scaling are confirmed by the Faust and sndkit docs and by Südholt et al. Verify the rest in your own source.
- The 1:6 epilarynx rule is an acoustic model (Sundberg 1974). Living-singer MRI does not always show it: Mainka et al. (PLOS ONE 2015) measured a +21.9% hypopharyngeal cross-section increase, while epilaryngeal changes "were not significant". Treat the epilarynx gene ranges as starting points.
- Adding anatomical detail does not guarantee better ratings. In Birkholz & Drechsel (2021), listeners could hear the piriform fossae and transvelar coupling but "less preferred" them, and with a more tense voice showed no preference either way.
- The ranked list is my judgment. It is based on the figure as described and on the literature, not on measurements of your model. Diagnostics 1, 2 and 5 should confirm or reorder the top five within a day.
